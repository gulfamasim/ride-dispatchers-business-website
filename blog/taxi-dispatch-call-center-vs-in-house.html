Taxi dispatch call center vs in-house desk

Five factors decide this, and cost is only one of them. Here is the framework we walk fleet owners through on a first call.

Ride Dispatchers Operations Team 8 August 2026 7 min read

We lose deals on this call reasonably often, and that is fine. Some fleets genuinely should keep dispatch in-house, and telling them so early saves everyone six wasted weeks. Here is the framework, including the parts that do not favour us.

The five factors

Cost, coverage, quality, control and risk. Owners usually arrive focused entirely on cost, and it is rarely the factor that decides the outcome.

1. Cost

An in-house dispatcher costs far more than their salary — employer NI, pension, holiday cover, sickness, recruitment, training, floor space, equipment, and your own management time. Continuous 24/7 cover needs roughly four and a half to five FTEs per concurrent seat once you account for shifts, rest and absence.

An outsourced desk in Pakistan typically lands up to 70% below that loaded figure, with recruitment, training, QA and management already inside the price. We break the full arithmetic down in our cost guide.

Advantage: outsourced, clearly, for any fleet needing more than one concurrent seat.

2. Coverage

This is where the argument is really won, and it is a scheduling fact rather than a sales claim.

Your hardest shift to staff is 11pm to 5am. Recruiting for it in the UK is difficult, retention is poor, and quality degrades because human beings are not good at 3am. Pakistan runs four to five hours ahead, so your overnight window is covered by agents working a normal daytime shift. Same clock hours for your riders, completely different staffing problem for us.

Flexibility compounds it. In-house headcount is fixed; you carry it through quiet Tuesdays to have it on busy Fridays. An outsourced desk rosters against your actual call curve.

Advantage: outsourced, decisively, if nights and weekends are where you hurt.

3. Quality

Genuinely mixed, and it splits cleanly.

In-house wins on local knowledge. A dispatcher who grew up in your town knows the shortcut, the pub with two entrances, and which estate the drivers will not enter after midnight. We train agents on your geography and lean on platform mapping, but a local starts ahead and stays ahead on the edge cases.

Outsourced wins on consistency. A managed desk brings documented SOPs, scripted escalation, monitored calls, monthly QA reviews and measured answer times. Most in-house desks have none of that — not from negligence, but because a fleet owner running the rota, the drivers and the vehicles has no capacity to build a QA programme.

Also worth stating plainly: consistency does not disappear when someone leaves. When your one great dispatcher resigns, your service level goes with them. On a managed desk, that is the provider's problem to absorb.

Advantage: depends on whether local knowledge or process consistency matters more to your work mix.

4. Control

In-house wins here and we are not going to pretend otherwise. Someone in the next room can be corrected in ten seconds. A remote team is directed through SOPs, a team lead and a weekly review — which is slower, and requires you to write down how you want things done.

That said, the control people imagine they have is often less than they think. If you are not listening to calls and not measuring answer times, proximity is not control; it is just proximity.

Advantage: in-house.

5. Risk

Different shapes of risk, not more or less of it.

In-house risk is concentrated in people: your dispatcher resigns, gets ill, or has a bad month, and there is no depth behind them. For a lot of small fleets, one resignation is a genuine operational crisis.

Outsourced risk is concentrated in the relationship and the data: provider dependency, a supplier that under-delivers, and cross-border data protection. All three are manageable — a signed DPA with the right transfer mechanism, named revocable platform access, defined service levels, and a documented exit plan — but they need doing properly rather than assumed. Our GDPR page covers how we handle the data side.

Advantage: even, provided the outsourcing is contracted properly.

The hybrid most fleets actually land on

After all that, the common answer is not either/or. It is:

  • Keep a small in-house daytime team for local knowledge, walk-ins, driver management and the relationships that need a face.
  • Outsource nights, weekends and overflow, where in-house economics are worst and recruitment is hardest.

You keep control and local expertise where they pay, and buy coverage where it is expensive to build. Most of our clients run exactly this shape, and nearly all of them arrived at it after starting with overflow only.

If you want to test it against your own numbers, send us 30 days of call data. If the answer is that you should keep it in-house, we will say so.
